# Entity Structure

The consortium needs a legal home before it can sign its first vendor contract. The working idea has been a 501(c)(3) nonprofit that negotiates consortium deals. This page examines that honestly — including why it probably doesn't work for us — and compares the realistic alternatives.

<div class="disclaimer">
  <strong>Planning analysis, not legal advice.</strong> Entity selection here involves tax-exemption law, antitrust exposure, and cannabis-adjacent regulation simultaneously. Retain nonprofit counsel before filing anything.
</div>

## The 501(c)(3) Question: Answered Honestly

### Why the idea is appealing

501(c)(3)s are the most familiar exempt form, can accept tax-deductible donations, and "nonprofit consortium" reads well to members and vendors alike.

### Why it likely fails for our purpose

**1. Private benefit / private inurement doctrine.** The organizing principle of 501(c)(3) is serving a *public charitable* purpose. Negotiating bulk discounts for a defined group of for-profit businesses delivers concentrated **private benefit** to identifiable insiders — precisely what [IRS enforcement guidance prohibits](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-lafa/den0455r.pdf). An organization whose principal activity is lowering costs for member businesses looks like a mutual-benefit club, not a charity. This is why real-world purchasing consortiums are almost never 501(c)(3)s.

**2. Commercial-activity character.** Contract negotiation, fee collection, rebate administration, and audit services are commercial functions. A 501(c)(3) conducting substantial non-charitable commercial activity risks both denial of exemption and unrelated business income tax (UBIT) on that income.

**3. Cannabis adjacency makes everything worse.** Even if the charitable purpose were otherwise defensible (say, "supporting small businesses"), an organization facilitating commerce in intoxicating hemp products sits near multiple tripwires: federal drug-policy restrictions in exemption practice, banking/payment-processor scrutiny, and grant-foundation incompatibility. A 501(c)(3) application describing THCA product purchasing aggregation would face elevated examination risk, and even approval would leave the entity fragile.

### Verdict

**Do not organize as a 501(c)(3).** It's the wrong tool: wrong fit for mutual-benefit purchasing, and the hemp adjacency multiplies the risk. The instinct behind it — a mission-driven steward rather than a profit-maximizer — survives into the alternatives below.

## The Realistic Alternatives

### Option A: 501(c)(6) Trade Association — the industry-standard answer

This is what most real purchasing groups actually are. Chambers of commerce, trade associations, and professional societies organize under §501(c)(6) as "**business leagues**" — organizations improving business conditions of a common industry line ([IRS guidance](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/other-non-profits/business-leagues)).

| Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Fit for negotiating member deals | ✅ Core permitted activity; GPO-style associations are commonplace |
| Tax-exempt | ✅ Federal income tax exempt |
| Member dues deductible | ✅ As ordinary business expenses (excluding lobbying portion) |
| Political/industry advocacy | ✅ Permitted (unlike c3) — valuable in a market facing regulatory change |
| Cannabis adjacency risk | ⚠️ Lower than c3 but nonzero; counsel should review the application language |
| Donations | ❌ Not deductible as charity (irrelevant for us) |
| Transparency duties | Annual Form 990 public disclosure |

**Concerns:** c6s may not engage in substantial *particular-member* services — activities whose primary beneficiary is one specific member. Group-wide negotiated contracts are general-benefit by design, which keeps this clean, but pricing individualized services (e.g., consulting for one shop) must stay minor.

### Option B: Cooperative Corporation (member-owned)

The Ace Hardware / True Value model: the members own the wholesaler/broker outright.

| Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Alignment | ✅ Strongest possible — entity literally belongs to members |
| Economics | Patronage dividends proportional to use; margins return to members |
| Antitrust posture | Strong tradition (Capper-Volstead analogues); established cooperative statutes in PA/NJ |
| Cannabis adjacency | ⚠️ Same sector risk as any structure; co-op status neither helps nor hurts much |
| Complexity | Higher: capital contributions, patronage accounting, state co-op filings |

Best when members want ownership and will contribute capital. Heavier lift at formation.

### Option C: Manager-Managed LLC (for-profit, possibly with a nonprofit affiliate later)

Simplest path to a signed contract: form an LLC owned by the founding team, negotiate contracts, charge fees or take admin percentages.

| Factor | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Speed & cost | ✅ Fastest, cheapest, most flexible |
| Credibility | ⚠️ "Why does this LLC get 2% of my purchases?" — harder governance story |
| Long-term | Members eventually resent a for-profit middleman they don't own — the historical pattern says this ends in either conversion to co-op/c6 or member exit |

A pragmatic sequence: launch as an LLC to prove the model, convert to 501(c)(6) or cooperative once volume justifies it. Many GPOs followed exactly this lifecycle.

### Comparison Table

| Dimension | 501(c)(3) | 501(c)(6) | Co-op Corp | LLC (launch) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Legal fit for group purchasing | ❌ Poor | ✅ Excellent | ✅ Excellent | ✅ Good |
| Formation complexity | High | Moderate | High | Low |

| Cannabis-adjacency risk | High | Moderate | Moderate | Moderate |
| Recommended? | **No** | **Yes — target end-state** | Yes, if members fund equity | **Yes — pragmatic start** |

## Conversion Mechanics: LLC → 501(c)(6) in Practice

If we launch as an LLC and convert, the sequence matters. Getting it backwards (nonprofit first, contracts second) wastes months; getting the transfer wrong creates tax exposure on appreciated value.

1. **Keep early contracts assignable or re-signable.** Vendor agreements should include change-of-entity assignment rights from day one — a standard clause that costs nothing at signing and everything later.
2. **Value transfer at conversion.** The LLC's contract book and member list are assets. Transferring them to a newly formed c6 should occur at cost/before material appreciation, ideally before major revenue — another argument for converting early rather than after the model is proven and valuable.
3. **The c6 formation itself:** articles + bylaws drafted around "improving business conditions of the hemp retail industry" language; Form 1024 filing with Form 8718 fee; no members' tax-deduction issues since dues are ordinary business expenses.
4. **Governance migration plan written into the LLC OA now:** how board seats convert, how founding-member economics sunset, who signs the transfer documents. Deciding this during friendly formation avoids the deadlock it causes later.

### State-law overlay

Pennsylvania and New Jersey each impose their own nonprofit/association regimes on top of the IRC sections: PA requires nonprofit incorporation through the Department of State with its own purposes test; NJ has analogous requirements plus public-charities registration only when soliciting public donations (we won't be, and NJ is outside our launch territory per the [execution plan]({{ '/execution-plan' | relative_url }}) — NJ-facing analysis retained for the eventual tristate expansion). ## Recommendation (Working Position)

1. **Launch:** manager-managed LLC among the founding team, operating under published, member-visible fee schedules and a written conflict-of-interest policy. Get two or three distributor contracts live.
2. **Grow toward:** conversion or parallel formation as a **501(c)(6) business league** (or member cooperative if Founding Members contribute capital), transferring the contracts and brand.
3. **Throughout:** annual independent review of vendor-fee transparency, antitrust counsel check-in on negotiation practices (the Capper-Volstead case studies show how expensive improvised coordination gets), and strict separation between consortium governance and any single member's commercial interests.
4. **Drop entirely:** the 501(c)(3) plan. Keep the *rhetoric* of stewardship; choose a vehicle where it's legally coherent.
