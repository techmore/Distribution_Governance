# Strengths & Weaknesses

An honest analysis of organized consortium purchasing versus the informal alternative — the shared monthly spreadsheet of preferred vendors and pricing — including why the spreadsheet pattern predictably fails in this market, and what weaknesses a formal consortium does *not* cure.

---

## Two Models Side by Side

| Dimension | Informal buying group ("the spreadsheet") | Organized consortium |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to start | Near zero | Formation + admin overhead |
| Speed | Immediate | Weeks–months to first contract |
| Pricing achieved | Whatever a vendor will verbally extend | Contracted tiers backed by volume commitments |
| Enforceability | None — quotes are revocable at will | Signed contracts with term, audit rights |
| Accountability for prices | Nobody's job | The entity's core function |
| Continuity | Dies when the volunteer loses interest | Institutional; survives individual turnover |
| Legal posture of coordination | Ambiguous; horizontal conduct among competitors | Entity negotiates independently; counsel-reviewed |
| Member trust | "Bob says this guy's good" | Vetted vendors, COAs, published fee schedules |
| Scalability | Degrades past ~10 participants | Designed for it |

---

## Why the Spreadsheet Won't Last

The spreadsheet isn't wrong because spreadsheets are bad. It fails on five structural grounds:

**1. No commitment behind the price.** A vendor extends "group pricing" to a spreadsheet group as *unpriced goodwill* — no volume floor, no term, no signature. When their capacity tightens or a bigger buyer appears, those quotes are the first thing to go. Nothing was ever actually sold.

**2. No audit mechanism.** Nobody verifies members actually receive the listed prices. Invoice drift, substitution, and quiet repricing accumulate invisibly. Within months the sheet lists prices nobody is paying.

**3. No maintenance economy.** Someone volunteers to update it monthly in a market where THCA product SKUs, processors, and prices churn weekly. That person is unpaid, unaccountable, and replaceable by nothing. When they stop (and they stop), the artifact is instantly stale — worse than none, because it *looks* authoritative.

**4. Free-riding without enforcement.** Members take the sheet's intel and negotiate privately, or buy wherever. The group never develops the aggregated leverage the sheet implies, so the prices on it were never real anyway. This is the classic collective-action failure: everyone wants the benefit, nobody can be compelled to contribute.

**5. Market flux outpaces manual curation.** This industry specifically — regulatory uncertainty, processor churn, volatile biomass pricing, constant new SKU forms — has an information half-life measured in days. A monthly-updated informal list is structurally incapable of tracking it. Formal consortia handle flux through contracted terms and dedicated administration; informality handles flux not at all.

Sean's read on this is correct and worth stating plainly: **a preferred-vendor spreadsheet is a snapshot of goodwill that exists until the first party finds it inconvenient.** Every long-lived example on the [precedents page]({{ '/case-studies' | relative_url }}) has a funded organization behind it. We found no durable counterexample.

## Strengths of the Consortium Model

- **Real pricing leverage.** Committed aggregate volume earns genuine wholesale tiers — bulk economics without bulk purchases, the model's whole point.
- **Distributor alignment, not extraction.** Fewer accounts, larger orders, predictable demand — distributors gain real operating efficiency. Deals hold because both sides profit.
- **Collective quality control.** One professional vetting process (COAs, licensure, insurance) replaces forty sloppy ones. In an unregulated-corner-of-the-market category, this may be worth as much as price.
- **Institutional memory and continuity.** Contracts, relationships, and market data live in the entity, not one person's head.
- **Legal clarity.** Negotiation conducted by an independent entity under counsel-reviewed agreements avoids improvised horizontal coordination among competitors.
- **Member services compound.** Rebate collection, price benchmarking, payment-term pooling, and regulatory monitoring all become possible once administration exists.

---

## Weaknesses of the Consortium Model — Stated Plainly

Advocacy without honesty is worthless. The organized model carries real costs:

1. **Overhead tax.** Admin fees (typically 1–3%) plus dues are a permanent cost. If negotiated savings don't clear overhead by a comfortable margin, members are net losers. Savings must be measured and reported annually.
2. **Slow by design.** Committees and contracts move slower than a phone call to a new vendor. In a hot category, members will chafe at process.
3. **Free-rider problem persists even formally.** Off-contract purchasing remains legal (and should), but every dollar routed outside weakens the next negotiation round. Mitigations: tier benefits tied to participation share, patronage-style rebates proportional to spend-through.
4. **Vendor capture risk.** If admin fees fund the organization, vendors paying the most gain influence over the catalog. Healthcare GPOs police this with mandatory fee disclosure and member committees; we must too, or member trust dies exactly like spreadsheet trust does.
5. **Governance drift.** Entities serve insiders unless checked. Founding-member advantages need sunset clauses.
6. **Antitrust discipline required forever.** Group purchasing sits near competition law. Price-fixing among members, boycotts of non-contract vendors, or market allocation are hard prohibitions needing annual counsel review — the Capper-Volstead mushroom-co-op litigation shows how one membership error becomes ruinous liability.
7. **Sector-specific fragility.** All of this is built on the current farm-bill hemp framework. The consortium survives market chaos better than a spreadsheet would, but no structure survives the underlying legality disappearing. Structure buys resilience, not immunity.
8. **Concentration creates dependency.** Members who route most purchasing through consortium contracts lose supplier diversity; if a contracted distributor fails (common in this market), the group scrambles collectively instead of individually. Contract diversification across 3+ distributors mitigates.

## When the Lightweight Approach IS Enough

For completeness — there are conditions under which a spreadsheet-style group is genuinely adequate:

- Category is stable and low-churn (not ours)
- Fewer than ~10 participants who all know each other
- Purchases are small relative to any single member's business
- No meaningful quality/compliance vetting burden
- Nobody needs the savings badly enough to fund administration

Our market fails nearly all five tests simultaneously. That convergence — high churn, many participants, serious compliance stakes, real money involved — is precisely the profile where history shows organizations forming.

## Design Consequences

The weaknesses above translate directly into requirements:

| Weakness | Required design element |
|---|---|
| Overhead tax | Annual audited savings report per member |
| Slow decisions | Fast-track pilot categories; delegated authority under spending caps |
| Free-riding | Participation-tied tier benefits and rebate share |
| Vendor capture | Mandatory fee disclosure; member committee controls catalog |
| Governance drift | Sunsets on founding privileges; staggered elected board |
| Antitrust exposure | Written antitrust policy; annual counsel review; no-member-contact negotiation rules |
| Sector fragility | Wind-down/asset-return provisions written into the formation docs now |
| Distributor failure | Minimum three contracted suppliers per major category |
