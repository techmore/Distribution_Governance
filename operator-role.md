# The Operator: Neutral Steward of the Consortium

Every consortium in the [historical record]({{ '/case-studies' | relative_url }}) eventually employed a professional administrator whose job is neither buying nor selling, but governing the space between. This page defines that role for our organization — why it must exist, who fills it well, and why the single most important qualification is *not owning a shop*.

---

## What the Operator Actually Does

The Operator (Administrator/Executive Director) runs the machinery documented across this site:

- Executes the monthly reconciliation loop ([mechanics here]({{ '/consortium-model' | relative_url }}))
- Runs RFQ rounds end-to-end: bid sheets out, scoring in, award memos drafted for committee decision
- Maintains vendor compliance files (COAs, licensure, insurance)
- Produces quarterly savings statements and spend-through reports per member
- Administers dues, rebates, and tier tracking
- Serves as the *single communication channel* between members and vendors on commercial terms

That last line is the load-bearing one. In the operator model, **members never negotiate with vendors, and vendors never court members** — all commercial traffic routes through one neutral professional. Compare the spreadsheet world: every shop-owner talking to every sales rep, with prices and side-deals flowing in every direction at once.

## Why Neutral Is Structural, Not Ethical

### Game theory 1: The agency problem

The Operator is an *agent* acting for many *principals* (the member shops). Classic agency analysis asks: when do agents serve principals instead of themselves? Two mechanisms keep agents honest:

1. **Alignment via compensation design** — pay the Operator primarily from mechanisms tied to *member outcomes* (salary + bonus keyed to audited member savings and retention), not from volume commissions or vendor-side incentives. A commission-on-purchases structure would make the Operator push members to buy more rather than save more — the wrong objective function entirely.
2. **Information symmetry** — the Operator publishes everything: fee schedules, award memos, exception logs. An agent who cannot hide information has little room to defect.

### Game theory 2: Why the Operator cannot own a shop

Suppose the Operator also runs a retail location. Now analyze their incentive set:

| Situation | Neutral Operator's move | Shop-owner Operator's move |
|---|---|---|
| Two vendors bid; one offers the owner-operator an unofficial personal discount | Award purely on score | Steer award toward their secret benefactor |
| Scarce product allocation among members | Pro-rata by commitment tier | Favor their own store first |
| Category selection for next RFQ | Highest member-demand category | Categories *their* shop buys most |
| Negotiation intel (vendors' floor prices) | Protected for the collective | Exploited for personal margin |
| Member recruiting | Grow the pool | Slow-roll competitors near their own location |

Each row alone is survivable; together they're fatal. And note the deeper problem: even a perfectly honest shop-owner Operator *cannot prove* honesty, because every member must permanently entertain the possibility of favoritism. In repeated-game terms, suspicion poisons cooperation even without actual defection — members under-invest (free-ride, withhold data, buy off-contract) because they can't verify the referee is neutral. The equilibrium unravels exactly as the [spreadsheet failure]({{ '/strengths-weaknesses' | relative_url }}) does, but with extra steps.

This is textbook **conflict-of-interest as mechanism failure**: it's not about punishing bad character, it's about designing roles where bad incentives don't exist. Every mature GPO reached the same conclusion — healthcare GPOs bar clinicians with supplier financial interests from contract committees; co-op boards recuse conflicted directors on vendor awards ([agreement Article VII]({{ '/membership-agreement' | relative_url }})). Our rule is simply the same principle applied to the highest-power role: **the Operator holds no ownership, employment, or revenue interest in any member or vendor business. Full stop.**

### Game theory 3: The liaison as repeated-game coordinator

Why does routing everything through one person help? Because consortium success is an iterated prisoner's dilemma:

- **Members** cooperate (route purchases through contracts) or defect (buy off-contract after seeing the price sheet)
- **Vendors** cooperate (honor contract pricing) or defect (quiet repricing, favoring big direct accounts)

One-shot interactions reward defection — this is precisely why informal groups collapse. But in a *repeated* game with observable behavior and credible consequences, cooperation becomes the rational strategy. The Operator manufactures those three conditions:

1. **Observability** → the reconciliation loop makes defection visible (price audits catch vendor drift; spend-through reports catch member free-riding)
2. **Credible consequence** → credit-memo enforcement on vendors, tier downgrade on members — administered uniformly, no exceptions
3. **Long shadow of the future** → multi-quarter contracts and annual membership make tomorrow's gains worth today's restraint

A neutral Operator is the *institutional embodiment* of the repeated game. Members cooperate not because they like each other but because the Operator's enforcement makes cooperation dominant. This is also why the Operator should be paid steadily and modestly rather than performance-explosively: you want the coordinator playing an infinite-horizon game, not harvesting a short one.

### Economics 4: Transaction cost reduction (Coase/Williamson)

Oliver Williamson's Nobel-cited framework: firms and institutions exist to minimize transaction costs — search, bargaining, contracting, monitoring, enforcement. A smoke shop buying THCA today incurs all five, badly, every month: hunting suppliers (search), haggling alone (bargaining), no real contracts (contracting), hoping COAs are real (monitoring), no recourse when batches fail (enforcement).

The consortium centralizes each cost once, professionally, and amortizes it across all members — which is just another proof of the core value claim: bulk pricing without bulk purchases applies to *transaction costs*, not only product price. The Operator is the fixed asset that makes this centralization possible. Without them, transaction costs revert to being borne individually — i.e., back to the spreadsheet world.

## Why the Role Benefits Each Party

| Party | What the neutral Operator gives them |
|---|---|
| Members | A referee with no dog in the fight; guaranteed their data isn't leaked to rivals; uniform enforcement so cooperators aren't suckers; one professional negotiation instead of forty amateur ones |
| Vendors | One sophisticated counterparty who speaks contract, pays on time administratively, and doesn't waste sales time courting 40 shops; confidence that allocations won't be gamed by an insider |
| Founders | Institutional continuity — the entity survives partner turnover; reduced personal legal exposure (negotiations run through policy-governed process, not ad hoc); a saleable, transferable operation |
| The entity itself | Clean separation of governance (board sets policy) from execution (Operator administers) — the standard nonprofit/trade-association control structure that keeps the eventual [501(c)(6) conversion]({{ '/entity-structure' | relative_url }}) defensible |

## Compensation & Hiring Profile

- **Profile:** procurement or association-management background; comfortable with spreadsheets-as-software early on; detail-obsessed; *no industry ownership stakes*. Cannabis-sector familiarity helpful, neutrality mandatory.
- **Structure:** base salary (from dues/admin fees) + annual bonus keyed to two numbers only: audited member savings delivered, and member retention. Never keyed to purchase volume, vendor count, or any vendor-paid metric.
- **Governance:** hired and reviewed by the board; Product Committee (member representatives) controls the catalog, Operator executes it — separation of catalog authority from administration prevents power concentration.
- **Transition note:** at launch this may be part-time work absorbed by a founding partner — acceptable *only if* that partner holds no retail interest and the conflict policy is written as though a stranger held the role. Build the rules for the professional you'll hire, then let the founder stand in temporarily under the same constraints.
